# Competitive Product Teardown

Understand how customers solve a job and why competitors make their choices. Avoid feature-count theater, imitation roadmaps, and unsupported claims about another company's strategy.

## Frame the decision

Establish:

- decision the teardown must inform, owner, and date;
- target customer, buyer, job, and buying situation;
- products and alternatives in scope, including DIY and doing nothing;
- lens: journey, product, positioning, pricing, GTM, business model, AI, or full system;
- geography, plan tier, device, time window, and access constraints; and
- what evidence would change the current belief.

If the user asks for “everything,” choose the smallest decision-relevant scope and state exclusions.

## Use an evidence ladder

Prioritize:

1. observed product behavior in an authorized account or public experience;
2. official product pages, documentation, pricing, policies, demos, and changelogs;
3. official customer stories, filings, and company statements;
4. dated customer reviews, forums, support discussions, and win/loss evidence;
5. reputable reporting, analyst material, and public ecosystem signals; and
6. inference, clearly labeled as inference.

For every material observation record source, capture date, plan or version, and confidence. Distinguish claimed capability, observed capability, and customer-reported experience. Do not invent access, bypass controls, scrape against terms, or represent a trial observation as universal behavior.

## Trace the same job

Compare how each alternative helps the same target customer move from trigger to outcome:

- discovery and message;
- evaluation, proof, and buying friction;
- onboarding and time to first value;
- core task and critical decision points;
- exception, failure, recovery, and support;
- collaboration, governance, and administration;
- repeated use, expansion, switching, and exit; and
- realized outcome and evidence visible to the customer.

Capture steps, effort, handoffs, information requirements, trust signals, delight, failure modes, and unresolved gaps. Do not infer customer emotion without research.

## Analyze the strategic system

Evaluate:

- target segment and category frame;
- promise, proof, and objection handling;
- packaging, value metric, price, discount visibility, and switching cost;
- acquisition and sales motion, partner role, and service model;
- capability, data, distribution, ecosystem, network, cost, or trust advantages;
- explicit trade-offs and customers the competitor appears willing not to serve; and
- likely reinforcing loops or structural constraints.

For AI or agentic products, also examine task boundary, autonomy, permissions, grounding, user control, latency, failure visibility, fallback, evaluation evidence, and cost-to-serve implications.

## Classify differences honestly

Use:

- **Table stakes:** needed to enter or remain credible for the target job.
- **Parity:** meaningfully equivalent for this segment, even if implemented differently.
- **Differentiator:** affects customer choice or realized outcome and has credible proof.
- **Strategic asymmetry:** derives from a capability or constraint competitors cannot or will not easily match.
- **Distraction:** visible difference with weak connection to the target outcome.
- **Unknown:** insufficient evidence.

A missing feature is not automatically an opportunity. Determine whether customers care, why it is absent, and whether the omission reflects a deliberate trade-off.

## Convert observations into choices

For each material finding state:

1. observation and evidence;
2. implication for the target customer or buyer;
3. plausible strategic explanation, labeled as inference;
4. response options: ignore, match, counter-position, partner, re-segment, or test;
5. trade-off and second-order effect; and
6. cheapest evidence that would improve the decision.

Recommend a small number of moves. Avoid “copy all strengths and fix all weaknesses.” Preserve strategic coherence and name what not to pursue.

## Required outputs

Deliver:

1. findings ordered by decision impact;
2. a completed [Competitive Teardown Canvas](references/competitive-teardown-canvas.md);
3. an evidence register with observed, claimed, reported, inferred, and unknown labels;
4. journey and strategic-system comparisons;
5. differentiated opportunities and explicit non-opportunities; and
6. a recommended response, next test, owner, and review trigger.

## Quality gate

Confirm that the competitors serve a comparable job and segment, the analysis includes alternatives and doing nothing, versions and dates are visible, claims are separated from observations, customer consequence matters more than feature count, strategic inference is labeled, pricing comparisons normalize units and tiers, missing features are not assumed to be opportunities, and each recommendation names a trade-off and validation step.

## Permission boundary

Use public or authorized sources. Do not create deceptive accounts, bypass access controls, violate terms, contact competitors or their customers, purchase products, publish accusations, or alter external systems without explicit authorization.
