# Market and GTM Strategy

Connect market learning to a specific product and investment decision. This is a product-led strategy skill, not a substitute for specialist execution of paid media, brand campaigns, public relations, or sales operations.

## Start with the decision

Establish:

- the decision, owner, deadline, and cost of delay;
- the proposed user outcome and business outcome;
- current belief and the evidence supporting it;
- the assumption most likely to change the decision;
- the time horizon, geography, product maturity, and resource boundary; and
- whether the work is exploration, validation, launch, expansion, or recovery.

If the request starts with a channel or launch tactic, reframe it around the customer behavior and commercial result the tactic is intended to create.

## Build an evidence register

Separate:

- **Observed:** directly supported by dated, attributable evidence;
- **Reported:** stated by a customer, prospect, seller, operator, or source;
- **Inferred:** a reasoned interpretation of multiple signals;
- **Assumed:** plausible but unverified; and
- **Unknown:** material information not yet available.

Prefer current primary sources, direct customer or buyer behavior, first-party product data, won and lost deal evidence, and observed alternatives. Record source date, population, method, and limitations. Do not turn directional research, synthetic personas, or a small convenience sample into a market fact.

## Understand the market

Define the market by the job and alternatives, not only by an industry label. Analyze:

1. user, buyer, economic buyer, approver, blocker, and operator;
2. triggering event, desired progress, current workaround, and switching cost;
3. direct competitors, adjacent solutions, services, internal tools, DIY, and doing nothing;
4. category maturity, structural change, regulation, technology, and why now;
5. segment differences in pain, urgency, access, willingness to pay, and retention potential; and
6. the company's relevant assets, constraints, credibility, and right to win.

Research behavior before stated preference. Look for payment, switching, repeated use, manual work, escalation, or budget evidence.

## Size without false precision

Use at least one bottom-up model and a second independent method when the decision warrants it.

- **TAM:** all relevant demand under a clear market definition.
- **SAM:** the portion reachable by the proposed product, geography, capability, and route to market.
- **SOM:** a time-bound, capacity-constrained share supported by access, conversion, retention, and competitive assumptions.

Show the formula, units, period, source, and sensitivity. Use ranges. Never present a percentage of a broad analyst market as an obtainable plan without a customer-count and revenue bridge.

## Choose the segment and wedge

Compare credible segments across:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Pain and urgency | Is the problem important enough to change behavior now? |
| Evidence | Do observed behavior and research support the need? |
| Access | Can the company reach buyers and users efficiently? |
| Value and economics | Is there measurable value and willingness to pay? |
| Product fit | Can the product deliver a complete outcome for this segment? |
| Retention or expansion | Does initial value create durable use or a growth path? |
| Strategic fit | Does the wedge build a capability, data, network, or credibility advantage? |

Recommend one initial segment, explicitly defer others, and name what evidence would change the choice.

## Design the market promise

Create a positioning hypothesis with:

- target segment and buying situation;
- high-priority problem or desired outcome;
- category or frame of reference;
- differentiated promise;
- credible proof; and
- the alternative the customer must stop using or combine differently.

Separate message from proof. A claim is not differentiated merely because competitors do not use the same words.

## Connect pricing and packaging to value

Define the value metric, payer, buying unit, package boundary, price hypothesis, and discount or services implications. Compare willingness-to-pay evidence, competitive anchors, delivery cost, gross-margin constraints, and sales complexity. Treat pricing as a testable product decision, not launch decoration.

## Choose the route to market

Compare product-led, sales-led, partner-led, community-led, and hybrid options based on buyer complexity, time to value, deal size, implementation effort, trust, and access. For each serious option state:

- entry point and target audience;
- customer action the channel should produce;
- activation and handoff path;
- owner and required enablement;
- expected cost, capacity, and feedback speed; and
- leading signal, outcome signal, and stopping rule.

Do not recommend a channel because it is popular. Explain why it matches this buyer and motion.

## Account for agentic-product adoption

For AI or agentic products, add:

- a task-level promise that can be demonstrated and evaluated;
- trust, security, privacy, procurement, and human-oversight requirements;
- the workflow and role change required to realize value;
- quality-floor, latency, and failure-recovery proof by target use case;
- inference, review, support, and exception-handling cost in the economic model; and
- data, model, tool, or platform dependencies that may weaken differentiation or margins.

Do not market general intelligence when the evidence supports only a bounded task. Connect the promise to observable task completion and authoritative product outcomes.

## Stage the launch as a learning sequence

Design the smallest sequence that can test the market thesis:

1. **Problem proof:** the segment demonstrates the problem through behavior or commitment.
2. **Value proof:** a bounded experience creates the promised outcome.
3. **Repeatability proof:** acquisition, activation, delivery, and retention work beyond a hand-held cohort.
4. **Economic proof:** pricing, servicing, acquisition, and retention can support the model.
5. **Scale proof:** the operating system and channel can grow without breaking quality or trust.

For each stage define population, offer, channel, owner, readiness criteria, primary metric contract, threshold, guardrails, review date, and expand/revise/stop decision. Include sales, customer-success, support, legal, finance, data, and operations readiness when relevant.

## Close the commercial learning loop

After launch, review product and commercial evidence together:

- message-to-response and channel-to-qualified-demand;
- sales cycle, win/loss reasons, objections, and buying-committee friction;
- activation, time to value, retention, expansion, and churn by segment;
- realized price, discounting, cost to serve, and gross-margin effects;
- promised versus realized customer value; and
- mix shift versus within-segment performance.

Distinguish product-market fit, channel fit, and commercial execution. A weak launch does not automatically disprove the product, and usage without sustainable acquisition or economics does not prove the market.

## Required outputs

Deliver:

1. a findings-first executive recommendation;
2. a completed [Market and GTM Decision Canvas](references/market-gtm-decision-canvas.md);
3. an evidence register with confidence and gaps;
4. a segment and route-to-market comparison;
5. a staged launch and learning plan; and
6. the next decision, owner, and review date.

## Quality gate

Confirm that the work begins with a decision, distinguishes user from buyer, includes alternatives and doing nothing, shows market-sizing math, selects rather than lists segments, connects positioning to proof, connects pricing to value, names route-to-market mechanics, separates evidence from assumptions, defines thresholds and guardrails, and includes post-launch commercial learning.

## Permission boundary

Read-only research and drafting are allowed when in scope. Do not contact customers, buy research, change pricing, launch a campaign, publish positioning, modify CRM records, or send enablement materials without explicit authorization.

## Method notes

This original operating contract combines decision-driven market research, product strategy coherence, staged product-market-fit learning, and commercial readiness. It was informed by public market-research skill patterns and Silicon Valley Product Group's emphasis on testing value and business viability before delivery; it does not reproduce a licensed framework or private employer process.
