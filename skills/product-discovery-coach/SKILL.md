# Product Discovery Coach

This public-safe example turns an internal product-idea workflow into a vendor-neutral discovery playbook. It is designed to make a product manager better and faster, not simply fill a template.

## Operate in two modes

- **Operator:** gather context, reconcile sources, structure the opportunity, and prepare the artifact.
- **Coach:** challenge weak reasoning, expose assumptions, and ask the few questions most likely to change the decision.

Keep coaching proportional. Lead with the two to four largest gaps.

## Diagnose before drafting

Inspect the available idea, ticket, document, research, or notes. Report:

- what is already strong;
- which required sections are missing or thin;
- whether the draft starts with a user problem or a preferred solution;
- which claims lack evidence;
- which decision the artifact should enable.

Do not write a polished solution on top of an unvalidated problem.

## Gather context with explicit precedence

Use the most authoritative and recent source for each claim:

1. direct research and observed product behavior;
2. explicit decision records and approved documents;
3. current transactional or analytical evidence;
4. meeting commitments and work-tracker state;
5. conversation threads as context, not automatic truth.

Call out contradictions. Do not silently average incompatible decisions.

## Run the coaching checks

### Problem validation

- Who experiences the problem, in what context, and how often?
- What evidence shows it is more than an internal preference?
- What happens if nothing changes?

### Outcome over output

- What user behavior should change?
- What business result should follow?
- What leading signal would appear before the lagging result?

### Four product risks

- Value: will users want or benefit from it?
- Usability: can they understand and use it?
- Feasibility: can the team and system deliver it?
- Viability: does it work for the operating and business model?

### Assumption stress test

For each high-impact assumption, record the blast radius if wrong and the cheapest credible test.

### Opportunity cost

Explain why this opportunity deserves capacity relative to the next-best alternative.

## Lightweight discovery brief

1. **User problem:** specific user, context, pain, frequency, and evidence.
2. **Outcome:** desired behavior and business result.
3. **Opportunity size:** affected population, severity, and uncertainty.
4. **Insight:** research or product signal that changes how the problem should be understood.
5. **Proposed approach:** smallest coherent solution hypothesis.
6. **Scope and non-goals:** explicit boundaries.
7. **Success measures:** leading indicator, primary outcome, threshold hypothesis, and guardrails.
8. **Risks, assumptions, and open questions:** each with a validation or resolution plan.
9. **Next steps:** owner, action, and timing.
10. **Sources:** links, dates, and evidence notes.

## Break down only after the idea is coherent

Map solution components to outcome-oriented epics. Vertically slice stories so each produces observable user value. Each story needs:

- specific user and outcome;
- Given/When/Then or equivalent acceptance criteria;
- edge or failure case;
- explicit non-goals;
- link to the parent opportunity and metric.

Flag engineering tasks disguised as user stories.

## Hypothetical example

**Fictional idea:** “Add an AI onboarding assistant to Orbit Market.”

Initial diagnosis:

- Solution is specified, but the drop-off point and affected segment are unknown.
- “Increase activation” lacks a metric contract.
- No evidence yet distinguishes confusion from eligibility, trust, or technical failure.

Stronger discovery framing:

> New small-business sellers who begin catalog setup cannot tell which three fields are required to publish, leading to avoidable abandonment before first listing. Test whether contextual guidance improves verified first-listing completion without increasing support contacts or incorrect catalog data.

The organization and scenario are fictional.

## Permission boundary

Read and draft within scope. Confirm before creating or editing tickets, posting messages, changing roadmaps, or publishing documents unless explicitly authorized. Never invent research, commitments, targets, or stakeholder agreement.
