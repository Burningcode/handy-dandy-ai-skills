# Craft and end-to-end review

Use the checks relevant to the work. This is a design baseline, not an accessibility certification, legal opinion, or substitute for the active organization's standards and accountable reviewers.

## 1. The person and the whole service

Name the functional, emotional, and social job, what just happened, and the person's constraints. Follow the journey beyond the screen through waiting, handoffs, support, completion, and recovery. A completed form is not necessarily a completed service.

At each meaningful handoff, identify the receiving owner, information carried forward, visible state, expected wait, next action, and recovery path. Do not let "submitted" imply "approved," "paid," or "delivered." Preserve work across ordinary interruptions when the actual security and retention rules permit it.

## 2. Choice, control, and truthful promises

Put decision-critical cost, eligibility, uncertainty, commitment, and consequences beside the action. Use progressive disclosure for supporting detail, not information whose absence changes the choice.

Match friction to consequence: undo can serve a recoverable action; serious or irreversible effects need a deliberate review and explicit confirmation. Preserve refusal, correction, exit, and recovery. Separate optional data uses from required service steps and collect only what the current job needs. Do not claim a consent or compliance decision has been approved because the interface is clear.

## 3. State and recovery

Distinguish first use, no results, completed work, permission limits, configuration problems, and system failure. Explain the actual state and the useful next step instead of showing a generic blank or blaming the person.

For errors, preserve valid input, identify the problem in words, connect the error to the relevant control, and provide a logical correction path. For uncertain external outcomes, show pending or reconciliation rather than inviting a duplicate consequential action.

For long collections, preserve filters, selection, position, and return context. Do not make the person reconstruct their work after inspecting one item.

## 4. Hierarchy, visual systems, and accessibility

Make the primary decision and next action apparent. Use task-recognizable titles and labels rather than internal vocabulary. Use spacing to express relationships before adding boxes and borders. Define typography, color, and spacing by their roles, then test real short, long, dense, and empty content.

Check persistent labels, keyboard order and visible focus, contrast, non-color status cues, small and zoomed views, reduced motion, and meaningful text alternatives. A polished screenshot does not establish accessibility. Inspect the implemented experience and use the organization's applicable standards and testing process.

Use diagrams only when they clarify an important relationship. Provide the essential entities, sequence, and meaning in text as well. A chart should not manufacture certainty: keep units, denominator, population, time window, missingness, and relevant uncertainty clear. Distinguish zero from unavailable or not applicable. Compare values on honest, consistent scales.

## 5. Multi-sided and operational experience

For each participant, name the immediate value, requested effort, control, and dependency. Make proposal, approval, decline, revision, authority transfer, and next ownership explicit. Do not reveal another participant's private reasoning or notes to make a shared state look complete.

Look for hidden costs: support work, repeated entry, reconciliation, setup permissions, training, or recovery pushed onto an operator. Future network value is not value available today. Identify what the initial participant can genuinely accomplish before the ecosystem is mature.

## 6. Research that changes a decision

Define the uncertain behavior and the decision the study will inform. Recruit actual or likely users against meaningful experience, role, access, and context; record important exclusions. Avoid presenting a convenience sample as representative.

Give a believable starting condition and desired outcome without teaching the interface path. Preserve the first uncoached attempt. Distinguish unassisted, assisted, abandoned, and falsely believed complete outcomes. In interviews, reconstruct a recent specific event; recalled behavior remains self-report unless independently observed or corroborated.

Keep a traceable chain from observation to interpretation, sample-bounded finding, consequence, and next decision. An isolated severe failure can matter without establishing prevalence. Internal agreement, simulated participants, and generated variants are not customer evidence.

## 7. Make, check, and learn

When edits are authorized, produce a concrete next version. Compare it with the intended outcome, known constraints, relevant states, and source facts. Inspect a render or actual interaction when possible; otherwise state exactly what was and was not checked.

Record what the accountable person accepted, rejected, deferred, or escalated. A good stopping point is a usable improvement plus a named uncertainty and next test—not an exhaustive findings list.
