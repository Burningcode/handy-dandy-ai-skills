# Arguments that survive questions

The analysis may be broad; the audience should not need to watch every step. Use the method backstage and the decision onstage. Do not turn six lenses into six slides by default.

## Argue

Establish the audience's decision, available time, required format, and legitimate concerns. Preserve the user's chosen thesis as a hypothesis to examine, not a conclusion to justify at any cost. If support is inadequate, propose a narrower claim or conditional recommendation and explain the change.

Build the shortest coherent chain:

1. **Ask and thesis:** the choice we want made, for whom, and why now.
2. **Reality:** the few facts that make the problem consequential, with uncertainty beside the claim it qualifies.
3. **Mechanism:** why the proposed behavior change should produce customer value and business value. Make the reasoning connecting evidence to conclusion explicit.
4. **Choice:** why this option beats the strongest alternative on a common outcome/economic basis; what we forgo.
5. **Proof and commitment:** the smallest justified commitment, failure condition, accountable owner, and next decision.

Adapt order and length to the audience. For each consequential claim preserve its evidence, reasoning, boundary, strongest objection, and response. Detailed sources and sensitivity calculations can sit in backup material, but a caveat that changes the decision stays in the main story. A headline must not be stronger than the evidence beneath it.

Use concrete people, stakes, and choices rather than generic strategy vocabulary. Ground = where and how we can win, not a label to memorize. A personal retrospective can illustrate a hypothesis; it cannot prove another company's diagnosis. Never retell private employment history in an external case without explicit permission.

## Casework

Respect the case's actual question, rubric, AI/browsing rules, clock, and source packet. Keep case facts separate from background research and assumptions. Do not silently import live market facts into a closed-book case or employer information into a hypothetical.

Preserve one customer behavior, one principal business result, the causal mechanism, a credible rejected option, and decision criteria. If inputs are absent, use a labeled conditional or symbolic model and identify the value that would change the ranking. Numbers chosen for an experiment are proposed thresholds, not historical baselines.

For compressed formats, retain the decision-critical comparison and proof gate before adding roadmap detail. If another case skill governs the session, respect its collaboration gates and artifact deadline.

## Rehearse

Default to one consequential objection at a time, letting the user answer before coaching. If asked for an uninterrupted review, provide a compact objection-and-repair set instead.

Choose a relevant lens (customer, commercial, execution, risk, or skeptical decision-maker). Label this a simulated perspective, never real stakeholder evidence or an independent panel. Ask the question most likely to overturn the recommendation, not a trivia question. Then assess:

- Did the answer address the objection directly?
- Did it distinguish evidence from belief?
- Did the mechanism and alternative comparison hold?
- Did it change the recommendation when warranted?
- Could the audience repeat the decision and its condition?

Offer a stronger answer grounded in the same evidence, or revise the thesis. Don't reward unsupported certainty or rhetorical evasions. Stop when the priority weakness has been repaired or the remaining evidence gap is explicit.

Before delivery, compare the presentation with the analysis: recommendation, confidence, causal claims, economics, alternatives, thresholds, owner, and ask must agree. More polished is not more proven.
